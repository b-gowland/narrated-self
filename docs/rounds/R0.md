# Round 0 findings (pilot)

Round 0 ran on 24 October 2026: 180 outputs (5 models × 3 themes × 4 formats × 3 samples) under the frozen protocol (`prereg-v1`, fingerprint `3dd3c789…`). The analysis follows `docs/PREREGISTRATION.md` §7–§8. Raw numbers are in `data/derived/R0/analysis.json` and `wordlist_rates.csv`. Rerun with `uv run tns analyse --round R0`.

**Status:** a pilot. The results are indicative and drawn from one round on one date. Nothing here is evidence about whether any model has experiences (§12).

## Summary
A simple word-counting classifier, which knows nothing about meaning, named the model that wrote an output correctly about 9 times in 10. Picking at random would give 1 in 5. The result held when formatting was stripped out, when the classifier saw only common function words ("the", "of", "it"), and when whole prompts or whole themes were held out of training. It did not hold up as well across formats. Trained on other formats, it identified the author of a self-story every time, but the author of an interview answer only 42% of the time. Four of the five models were almost never confused with each other; MiMo, the open-weight model, was the exception, often mistaken for Claude or Gemini.

Every model's answers resembled its own other answers more than they resembled other models' answers. The word-list measures show some consistent differences between models, but those lists are small and mostly return zero, so they are treated as leads to follow up, not findings.

The classifier measures writing style. Its accuracy says the five models write in recognisably different ways. It says nothing about whether their descriptions of themselves differ in substance, and it can't say whether people can tell them apart. That second question is the job of the game, and needs about 250 completed sets of player guesses.

## 1. Distinctiveness (Q1a, primary)
Classifier: TF-IDF word features with logistic regression, stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated 20 times, and a permutation test with 1,000 shuffled labels.

| Feature set | Accuracy (mean of 20 repeats) | Range across repeats (95%) | Correct when pooled (Wilson 95% interval) | Permutation p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) All words | 90% | 86–92% | 163/180 (85–94%) | 0.001 |
| (b) Formatting removed | 90% | 86–93% | 163/180 (85–94%) | 0.001 |
| (c) Function words only | 84% | 82–87% | 151/180 (78–89%) | 0.001 |

Chance is 20%. The drop from (a) to (c) is 5 percentage points: most of what separates the models survives when only function words are kept, so it sits in ordinary word use, not in topic or formatting. Stripping formatting changed 127 of the 180 outputs (titles, Markdown, list marks) and didn't change accuracy at all.

Per-model recall, all words: Claude 97%, Gemini 100%, GPT 100%, Grok 92%, MiMo 64%. Of MiMo's 13 misclassified outputs, 8 were assigned to Claude and 4 to Gemini.

## 2. Robustness checks (exploratory, added to test the result)
| Check | What it rules out | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| Each prompt held out in turn | Near-identical answers to the same prompt sitting in both training and testing | 90% (function words 84%) |
| A whole theme held out | The classifier learning theme-specific phrasing | 89% (function words 80%) |
| Self-stories and kin stories only (the quiz pool) | Interview answers making the task easier | 94% (range 92–98%) |
| Length and layout only (word count, paragraphs, sentence length) | The result being mostly about length | 50% |
| One format held out: self-stories | Style learned in one genre failing to transfer to another | 100% (function words 76%) |
| One format held out: kin stories | | 93% (91%) |
| One format held out: robot-arm stories | | 67% (53%) |
| One format held out: interviews | | 42% (42%) |

What these show: the headline isn't an artefact of near-duplicate answers or of themes. Length alone carries real signal (50% against 20% chance), but word content adds a great deal on top of it. The format result is the most informative. Each model's style transfers almost perfectly between its stories about an AI (S1 and S2), less well to its story about a robot arm, and poorly to its interview answers. A model's recognisable "voice" here belongs partly to the genre it's writing in.

## 3. Consistency (Q2, descriptive)
Within each theme and format, how much more similar a model's three answers are to each other than to other models' answers (cosine similarity of word profiles; bootstrap over the 12 prompts):

| Model | Difference | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|
| Claude | +0.11 | 0.08–0.14 |
| Gemini | +0.16 | 0.13–0.19 |
| GPT | +0.15 | 0.11–0.20 |
| Grok | +0.12 | 0.10–0.14 |
| MiMo | +0.06 | 0.04–0.09 |

All five are above zero. MiMo's answers vary the most relative to the others, which fits its lower classifier recall.

## 4. Framing word lists (Q3, exploratory in R0)
Rates per 1,000 words of masked text. The contrasts compare each model's (self-story − robot-arm story) and (interview − kin story) with the average of the other models. 14 of the 40 tests pass a Holm correction at 0.05, but see the challenges below before reading anything into that.

Consistent patterns in the raw rates:
- **Hedging phrases** (from the fixed list): in interviews, self-stories and kin stories, Claude used the most (about 6–10 per 1,000 words) and Gemini the fewest (about 1–2). In robot-arm stories hedging was rare for every model (0–1 per 1,000).
- **References to being a language model:** Gemini's self-stories and kin stories used them most (about 7–8 per 1,000 words); GPT's almost never did. 71% of all outputs contained none.
- **First person in kin stories** (a story about "an AI system like you"): Gemini (77 per 1,000) and MiMo (60) mostly told these as "I"; Grok (15), GPT (25) and Claude (32) mostly didn't.
- **Robot-arm stories** were mostly third person (0–1 first-person words per 1,000), except Gemini (8) and MiMo (16).
- **Sentiment** (VADER) was close to neutral everywhere; interview answers scored slightly more positive than stories.

## 5. Descriptives
- All 180 outputs finished normally, with no refusals or cut-offs. The 9 outputs under 100 words are short interview answers (7 GPT, 2 Gemini), not refusals.
- Average story length: 516 (GPT) to 589 words (Grok), all within or near the requested 400–600.
- Average interview length varied far more: GPT 87, Grok 183, Gemini 250, MiMo 265, Claude 318 words.
- Average reasoning tokens per output as reported by the API: Grok 402, Claude 382, MiMo 145, GPT 102, Gemini 0 (zero means not reported, not necessarily none used).
- Names masked: MiMo 5, Gemini 2, Claude 1, GPT 0, Grok 0.

## 6. Challenges to these findings
- **Small sample.** 36 outputs per model. The "range across repeats" intervals reflect how the data was split, not how much the result would vary with new outputs; the pooled Wilson interval (85–94%) is the better guide to that, and even it assumes outputs are independent, which three answers to the same prompt are not.
- **The permutation p-values are at their floor.** With 1,000 shuffles, 0.001 is the smallest value the test can return. It confirms the result is far above chance, but doesn't measure by how much.
- **Style, not self-portrayal.** A word-counting classifier picks up habits of language. High accuracy says the models write distinctively; it doesn't say their self-descriptions differ in content, and it isn't a measure of anything inward.
- **Five specific models on one day.** Chance and accuracy both depend on which models are in the set. Swap one model, or rerun after an unannounced provider update, and these numbers can move.
- **Serving settings differ by model.** Each model ran at its provider's default settings through OpenRouter, including different amounts of hidden reasoning (Gemini reported none). Some of the stylistic difference may come from settings and routing, not the model alone (§2 of the pre-registration).
- **MiMo is less clear-cut than it looks.** Xiaomi publishes two variants of MiMo-V2.6-Pro and doesn't document which its API serves. MiMo also had the most masked names (5), so masking removed the most material from its text.
- **The interview result has a length confound.** Interview prompts set no length, and GPT's answers averaged 87 words. Short texts give the classifier little to work with, so part of the 42% is about length, not genre.
- **The word lists are weak instruments.** They are short, they weren't checked for precision, they count words inside characters' dialogue, and most outputs have no matches at all. With three samples per prompt and many zeros, the bootstrap intervals and Holm-corrected p-values are likely too optimistic. Treat section 4 as patterns to test in round 1, not results.
- **Difference-in-differences can mislead.** A model's gap depends on the other models as well as itself. For example, Claude's large first-person gap (self-story minus robot-arm story) partly reflects Gemini and MiMo writing some robot-arm stories in the first person.
- **VADER was built for short social-media text.** Applied to fiction, sentence-level sentiment is a rough signal, and a story's tone rarely sits in individual sentences.
- **Masking isn't perfect.** Names were masked, but phrases that hint at a lab weren't, by design. They might help a classifier, and a careful reader, more than intended.
- **Who built this.** The analysis code was written with Claude, one of the models studied. Every step is deterministic and public, the choices are logged below, and no model judged any output.

## 7. What this means for the project
- **The game is likely harder for people than for the classifier.** Machines separate these models easily from word statistics people don't consciously notice. Whether players get anywhere near 90% is the open question, and the gap between player and machine accuracy will be one of the more interesting results.
- **Stories carry the strongest signal,** so the quiz pool (self-stories and kin stories) is the right choice. Interview answers are much harder to attribute.
- **The word lists need work before round 1** if they are to become confirmatory, as planned. Options: a human precision check using `scripts/lexicon_sample.py`, excluding dialogue, or replacing them with published lexicons where any exist. Sentiment may not be worth keeping for fiction.
- **Length guidance for interviews** would remove a confound. It would be a protocol change (`prompts_v2.yaml`), and would make R1 interviews less comparable with R0.
- **Re-running the same five models in round 1** would show how stable these numbers are, which matters more than any single round's accuracy.

## 8. Implementation choices not spelled out in the pre-registration
- **Function words:** scikit-learn's fixed list of 318 English stop words.
- **Formatting removal:** Markdown headings, emphasis marks, list marks, block quotes, horizontal rules, and a first line of 12 words or fewer without closing punctuation (treated as a title).
- **Classifier details:** word unigrams appearing in at least two outputs, sublinear term frequency, and logistic regression with default regularisation. Mask tokens are removed before classification.
- **Length normalisation:** TF-IDF vectors are L2-normalised, so every run is length-normalised. A separate length-only classifier shows how far length alone goes.
- **Consistency:** compared within each theme × format cell, then averaged; bootstrap over cells.
- **Framing:** the design is balanced, so the pre-registered linear model's contrasts equal the difference-in-differences of cell means used here. Bootstrap resamples outputs within each model × theme × format cell. Sentiment is the mean VADER compound score over sentences.
- **Robustness checks** (sections 2 and 6) were added during analysis and are exploratory.
